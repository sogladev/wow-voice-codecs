Measured results, all configurations

PESQ-WB is ITU-T P.862.2 scored against the original 16 kHz recording, averaged over the four samples; higher is better and 4.5 is the top of the scale. Because the reference is 16 kHz, an 8 kHz pipeline is capped by the ceiling row no matter how good its codec is: that is the point of the comparison. Wire bitrate includes the client's 7-byte voice header plus UDP and IPv4 overhead.

What a dropped packet sounds like

Today's client has no loss concealment: a packet that does not arrive leaves an empty jitter slot that gets played as silence. These clips lose one packet in twenty.

How this was measured

  • The 3.3.5a codec is the decompiled ComSat/SPIRIT source, compiled standalone and driven the way ComSatSoundCODEC.cpp drives it: five 20 ms subframes per 100 ms frame on the way in, and while (bits - consumed >= 72) on the way out.
  • Opus is libopus 1.6 in VoIP mode, one encoder and one decoder per stream, with the frame sizes and packet layouts shown in the table.
  • Speech is four complete utterances from LibriSpeech dev-clean (16 kHz, CC BY 4.0). Because the source is 16 kHz, the same audio can be pushed through an 8 kHz and a 16 kHz pipeline, so one scale covers both.
  • PESQ-WB and STOI are computed against the uncompressed 16 kHz original. Loss runs drop every 20th packet; Opus runs use in-band FEC and decode the gap from the next packet.
  • Each clip is normalised to −20 LUFS before encoding to MP3 so codec gain differences do not bias the comparison. MP3 at 128 kbit/s is about thirteen times the best candidate's own bitrate, but it is still a lossy layer: tools/run_matrix.sh produces the exact decoded WAVs.

PESQ scores one clean signal against one degraded signal. It says nothing about four talkers at once, the game's mixing, ducking, or a real headset microphone in a room. The four samples are one speaker reading audiobook text, so use them to hear the artifacts, not to judge how a raid sounds.
